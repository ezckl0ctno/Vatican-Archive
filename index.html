02 — Catholic Theology

The One and Holy Apostolic Catholic Church

Catholic theology teaches that the Church was founded by Christ, animated by the Holy Spirit, and marked by unity, holiness, catholicity, and apostolicity. The Catechism treats these as inseparable characteristics of the Church and her mission (CCC 811–812).

Trinitarian Theology

The Holy Trinity

Catholic faith confesses one God in three divine Persons: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. The Persons are distinct, yet the divine nature is one.

CCC232–267
ScriptureMt 28:19; 2 Cor 13:13; Jn 1:1–3
Christology

The Incarnation

The eternal Son truly became human without ceasing to be divine. Jesus Christ is one divine Person with a true divine nature and a true human nature.

CCC456–483
ScriptureJn 1:1, 14; Phil 2:6–8; Col 2:9
Sacramental Theology

The Eucharist

The Eucharist is the memorial of Christ's Passover and the sacramental making-present of his one sacrifice; Catholic teaching confesses Christ's real presence in the sacrament.

CCC1322–1419
ScriptureLk 22:19–20; Jn 6:51–58; 1 Cor 11:23–29
Ecclesiology

Petrine Ministry

Catholic teaching sees a unique pastoral office entrusted to Peter and continued in the ministry of the Bishop of Rome, exercised within the communion of the bishops.

CCC880–887
ScriptureMt 16:18–19; Lk 22:31–32; Jn 21:15–17
Mariology

Mary, Mother of God

Because Jesus Christ is truly God and truly man in one Person, the Church calls Mary Theotokos—Mother of God—referring to the identity of the Person she bore.

CCC484–511
ScriptureLk 1:28, 35, 43; Gal 4:4
Revelation

Scripture & Tradition

Catholic doctrine holds Sacred Scripture and Sacred Tradition together as modes by which the apostolic deposit of faith is handed on, authentically interpreted by the Church's teaching office.

04 — Historical Theology

The first seven ecumenical councils

These councils were decisive and crucial as it was the foundation of Trinitarian theology, Christology, and the theology of sacred images. Select a council to expand its historical context, doctrinal issue, and sources.

Central controversy: Arian teaching concerning the Son's relation to the Father.

Doctrinal importance: The council confessed the Son as truly divine and “consubstantial” (homoousios) with the Father, forming the foundational Nicene creed.

Biblical frame: Jn 1:1–3; Jn 10:30; Col 1:15–20; Heb 1:1–4.

Historical references: Vatican CTI, Jesus Christ, Son of God, Savior: 1700th Anniversary of Nicaea (2025); CCC 465.

Central controversy: Continuing Trinitarian disputes, especially denial of the full divinity of the Holy Spirit.

Doctrinal importance: It reaffirmed the Nicene faith and articulated the Church's confession of the Holy Spirit as Lord and giver of life.

Biblical frame: Mt 28:19; Acts 5:3–4; 1 Cor 2:10–11; 2 Cor 13:13.

Historical references: John Paul II, A Concilio Constantinopolitano I (1981); Vatican CTI, Theology Today §25.

Central controversy: How Christ's divinity and humanity are united, and whether Mary may rightly be called Theotokos (“God-bearer” / Mother of God).

Doctrinal importance: The council defended the unity of Christ's Person and upheld the title Theotokos as a Christological confession.

Biblical frame: Jn 1:14; Lk 1:35, 43; Gal 4:4.

Historical references: John Paul II, A Concilio Constantinopolitano I (also commemorating Ephesus); CCC 466.

Central controversy: The relation between Christ's divine and human natures after the Incarnation.

Doctrinal importance: Chalcedon confessed one and the same Christ in two natures, divine and human, united in one Person without confusion or division.

Biblical frame: Jn 1:1, 14; Phil 2:6–8; Heb 2:14–17.

Historical references: Pius XII, Sempiternus Rex Christus (1951); CCC 467.

Central controversy: Continuing disputes over how Chalcedon's Christology should be interpreted, especially the “Three Chapters.”

Doctrinal importance: The council reaffirmed the unity of the divine Person of Christ and sought to protect an orthodox reading of Chalcedon.

Biblical frame: Jn 1:14; Col 2:9; 1 Tim 2:5.

Historical references: Vatican CTI, Theology Today §25; CCC 468.

Central controversy: Monothelitism—the claim that Christ has only one will.

Doctrinal importance: The council taught that Christ possesses a divine will and a human will, corresponding to his two natures, with the human will freely harmonious with the divine.

Biblical frame: Lk 22:42; Jn 6:38; Heb 10:5–10.

Historical references: Vatican International Theological Commission, Select Questions on Christology (1979); CCC 475.

Central controversy: Iconoclasm and the legitimacy of sacred images.

Doctrinal importance: The council defended the veneration of sacred images while distinguishing veneration from the adoration owed to God alone; its Christological logic rests on the reality of the Incarnation.

Biblical frame: Col 1:15; Jn 1:14; Ex 25:18–22 (sacred imagery); distinction from idolatry: Ex 20:4–5.

Historical references: John Paul II, Duodecimum Saeculum (1987); CCC 476–477, 1159–1162.

05 — Historical Map

From the apostolic Church to Vatican City

A compact orientation to major periods. This is not intended as a complete history of Christianity.

c. 30–100

Apostolic Age

The Christian proclamation spreads from Jerusalem throughout the Roman world. Acts and the New Testament letters witness to early communities, bishops/overseers, presbyters, deacons, sacraments, and apostolic teaching.

2nd–3rd c.

Early Fathers

Writers such as Ignatius of Antioch, Justin Martyr, Irenaeus, Tertullian, Cyprian, and others testify to developing theological vocabulary and ecclesial structures.

4th–8th c.

Patristic Councils

The first seven ecumenical councils clarify Trinitarian and Christological doctrine and address the theology of sacred images.

1054 onward

East–West Separation

Longstanding theological, political, cultural, and jurisdictional tensions culminate in deep separation between Rome and Constantinople, though the history is more complex than a single date.

16th c.

Reformation & Trent

Western Christianity experiences major rupture. The Council of Trent responds doctrinally and reforms aspects of Catholic discipline and clerical formation.

1869–1870

Vatican I

The council addresses faith and reason and defines papal primacy and papal infallibility under specific conditions.

1929

Vatican City State

The Lateran Treaty establishes Vatican City State as a sovereign entity distinct from, and serving the independence of, the Holy See.

1962–1965

Vatican II

The council issues major constitutions and decrees on the Church, revelation, liturgy, the modern world, ecumenism, religious liberty, and more.
